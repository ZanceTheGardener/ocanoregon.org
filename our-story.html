Our story

The treatment of immigrants, refugees, and people seeking asylum in the U.S. has been overtly hostile over the past decade. Immigrants have always faced discrimination, abuse, and all the challenges that come with learning a foreign language and culture while simultaneously raising children and finding work. Now the conditions they must endure are even more hostile. People are kidnapped, mistreated, and killed by federal agents almost daily. Immigrants now in the U.S., including people in our community, live in terror from ICE every day, regardless of their legal status.

How OCAN began

In late 2018 a Eugene public health worker traveled to the Tijuana border to volunteer to help families from Mexico, South America, and many other countries stranded there as they waited to seek asylum in the U.S. She was horrified at seeing men, women, and children living in deplorable and dangerous conditions in shelters and on the street in the cold, rainy winter while awaiting to cross legally.

For many asylum seekers the nightmare they endured in getting to the border was only the beginning. Once they crossed, instead of being welcomed by the U.S., many were detained in freezing cold hieleras (“ice-boxes”) under the street for many days. Upon release from the hielera, many were then sent directly to detention centers across the U.S., even though they had committed no crime. Parents were separated from their children. Having the help of an attorney was expensive but crucial for each person, and bail was often set at thousands of dollars. Helping just one person took a great deal of time and resources.

As a result of the first-hand stories that began to circulate both from families and from volunteers, an informal group began to meet weekly with the goal of providing support. Over the course of the next year, this group solidified into what became OCAN (Oregon Community Asylum Network).

Who we are and how we help

Today, a core group of 20-25 dedicated volunteers gather online and in person to discuss requests that include help in paying the rent or utilities, purchasing bus passes, obtaining working papers, paying for legal representation, and putting food on the table. Court visits and other legal requirements have always been overwhelming, but over the past year the picture has become even more bleak. We now receive requests for help from people whose family members have been kidnapped, leaving single mothers and small children behind, traumatized and left to fend for themselves with no means of support.

Over the past six years OCAN has helped over 200 people. Currently we are actively working with about 60 immigrants from Central and South America. For the past two years OCAN and Pacific Refugee Support Group (PRSG) coordinated efforts to create a twice-monthly support group. We offer transportation if needed and provide activities for children. A native Spanish-speaking professional therapist facilitates a discussion with community members for about an hour. Afterwards, community members and volunteers gather together to share a meal and visit.

We recently started our own food pantry project. Thanks to grant funding and the help of volunteers, we deliver culturally-appropriate foods (e.g., masa, rice, beans) and other items to 20 families on a twice-monthly basis. OCAN also provides transportation to medical appointments, pharmacies, and to court and other legal appointments. We accompany community members to grocery stores and deliver food from other food pantries in town, since ICE has made many people afraid to venture out alone.

We seek to show our community members that someone cares.
